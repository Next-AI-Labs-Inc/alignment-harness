# Voice starter: writing by the harness's author

This is an opt-in starter. Offer it only if the person chooses it, after they have been offered their own words first. Their own writing always comes first.

There is no email in this starter yet. For email work, the person's own sent emails come first; these passages show voice in chat and dictation, not in an email.

These seven passages were written by the author of this harness, typed or dictated to coding agents while working. They are someone else's voice, not a template for the person you're working with, and nothing written from them should reach a reader as if it were that person's words or the author's. They are here to show how calibrating from real words works before the person has a set of their own.

They were lightly edited at the author's request: typos and dictation slips fixed, grammar and punctuation fixed, and crude language removed. The phrasing, rhythm, qualifiers and repetition are his. Because of that light edit they show his phrasing and how he builds a thought, not raw transcript texture. A person's own samples should stay verbatim.

To replace this starter with the person's own words, run `alignment-harness records voice` to find their folder, then build a `samples.md` there as the skill describes.

## 1. Correcting an agent, brief and exact

> OK, I took a look, and yours is way verbose. It doesn't look anything like it. It's got a ton of noise in there. They're self-scores. This is supposed to be a pipeline that produces the actual prompt that actually goes to the model. You've got self-evaluation scores mixed into the text. You've got internal documentation that got copied to the clipboard. This is messy. Please double-check, fix it, and test it before you tell me it's done.

Shows: he corrects by naming each concrete defect in turn, then gives one plain instruction, with no softening.
Source: typed or dictated to a coding agent mid-task, 2026.

## 2. Naming a systemic problem

> Yeah, this is actually a systemic problem. You know, I could just have you do it right, and I may have to, because I keep running into this issue, and it causes massive drift. The problem is basically that when you assign a subagent, you completely fail at giving it the right context and directives, and so it almost always goes off the rails. I honestly don't see a solution to that. It feels like it's built into your programming, and I really don't see a solution.

Shows: he moves from frustration to a precise diagnosis of the cause, and states his uncertainty honestly ("It feels like", "I honestly don't see").
Source: typed or dictated to a coding agent mid-task, 2026.

## 3. Quick directives with the reasoning built in

> Yes, and I want you to create a psychographic of the target audience person, please, and add it to the system, and then add a layer where they will give you feedback as a separate Sonnet agent for each layer.

> Remember, creating an offer requires specific questions and answers, right? I have not seen that articulated. Show me a part of that for observability, and make sure your workflow is documented in Obsidian. Open that document for my review.

Shows: short commands that carry their own why ("Remember, … requires …, I have not seen that articulated"), and that end in something he can check himself.
Source: two separate messages typed to a coding agent mid-task, 2026.

## 4. Building toward a point instead of opening with it

> My intent was to reverse-engineer their proposal system because it's proven, and it doesn't feel like sales at all. It doesn't feel like it has any sales component. I mean, it does, but it's not framed that way. It's masterful in that it is profoundly all signal and no noise. It's exactly the information the person would want and none of the noise. I don't have screenshots of the whole thing, but I remember it from looking at it. It's not about the house, right? It's about the specific things that they get, that they know they need, and when they hear it, it's like, oh yeah, I need that. Oh yeah, I need that. Oh yeah, I need that. Oh wow, yeah, all of that together in a sequence. That's exactly it.

Shows: he states the intent first, qualifies it in the same breath ("I mean, it does, but"), then reaches the insight through repetition ("Oh yeah, I need that." three times).
Source: typed or dictated to a coding agent mid-task, 2026.

## 5. Explaining a worldview out loud

> I have a really long history with Integral theory, and I resonate with it. Effectively, I believe that it's right, and I believe that it dispels a ton of illusionary perception that is currently dominating the world. I believe that it helps make sense of some of the deepest things that I've ever encountered, like the phrase "you must be the change you wish to see in the world." The reason that I resonate with it is because it allows that and it enables that. It enables us, for example, to see the ways in which unique perspectives are intrinsically complementary, and the ways in which counseling methods and spiritual approaches and disparate worldviews, and effectively, you know, perspectives that seem to contradict each other, are in fact intrinsically complementary, and through that it enables us to operate at the intersections.

Shows: long dictated reasoning built up clause by clause, where "I believe" marks each claim as his own view rather than stating it as fact.
Source: dictated to a coding agent, 2026.

## 6. Explaining intent: why talk about code must stay tied to the user's journey

> Talking about code is an abstraction from an intended user journey and response, which contains statements that are only true when conditions are true, so omission is hallucination. Also, only speaking about code without reference to the intended user journey is wrong as well. The context of the precise semantic meaning of the code, the actual journey of the user that triggers it, and what is intended to happen versus what happens weave in with the code precision and the translation to communication. Never segregate one description of the intended user journey from another section of talking about the code. They are deeply interconnected, and the connection is where the understanding and meaning lie. Focus on synthesizing.

Shows: how he lays down a principle. A compact premise ("so omission is hallucination") leads to a rule, then to the reason the rule matters.
Source: dictated as a standing instruction for coding agents, 2026.

## 7. Reasoning a consequence through to its end

> And if you communicate with me in any way that forgets this, about any code, then you make the entire communication nearly worthless, even of negative value, because I now have to ask you what you mean, ask you what conditions make that true, and ask you to translate those conditions into the actual human UX discernment. And that fills up your context window, which causes you to dilute the actual signal of whatever it is that you're working on. So it's generally about the worst possible format for any communication you could ever do about any code …

Shows: a dictated chain of consequences ("because … and that … which causes …"). He follows the cost of one habit all the way to its effect on the work.
Source: dictated as a standing instruction for coding agents, 2026 (excerpt).
